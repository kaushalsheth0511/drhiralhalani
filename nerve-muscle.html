Special interest

Disorders of nerve and muscle

This is the group of conditions Dr. Hiral trained an extra year for.

When to think of it

Weakness is a symptom, not a diagnosis.

A nerve and muscle assessment is worth arranging when any of the following is true.

  • Difficulty getting up from the floor or climbing stairs
  • Weakness of both hands or feet, or a foot that drags
  • Burning, tingling or numbness that is getting worse
  • Drooping eyelids, double vision, or a fading voice
  • Muscle pain with a raised CPK
  • Muscle wasting, twitching or frequent cramps
  • Weakness progressing after a fever or loose motions
  • A child who runs differently or falls often

Conditions treated

From the muscle fibre to the nerve root.

MuscleMyopathy

Disease of the muscle itself. Typically difficulty rising from the floor or a chair, climbing stairs, or lifting the arms above the head, without numbness. Causes range from thyroid disease and vitamin D deficiency to statin-related, inflammatory, metabolic and inherited myopathies — the treatable ones are common and are missed when weakness is written off as ageing or weakness of the mind.

MuscleMyositis — inflammatory muscle disease

Dermatomyositis, polymyositis, immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy and inclusion body myositis. The immune system attacks muscle, often with a rising CPK, sometimes with a rash, joint pain, breathlessness or difficulty swallowing. Most respond to immunosuppressive treatment, and some are associated with an underlying cancer that needs to be looked for — which is why the diagnosis needs to be exact, not approximate.

MuscleMuscular dystrophy

Inherited muscle disease — Duchenne and Becker, limb-girdle, facioscapulohumeral and myotonic dystrophy. Even where no cure exists, an exact genetic diagnosis changes real things: heart and breathing surveillance that prevents sudden deterioration, safe anaesthesia planning, eligibility for trials and new therapies, and accurate counselling for the next pregnancy in the family.

NerveGuillain–Barré syndrome (GBS)

A medical emergency. Weakness that starts in the legs and climbs upward over days, often after a diarrhoeal or viral illness, sometimes with tingling and difficulty walking. Breathing and swallowing muscles can be involved within a day or two, so this is treated as an admission — with IVIg or plasma exchange, close monitoring of breathing capacity, and ICU support where needed. Most patients recover well when treatment starts early.

NerveCIDP and immune neuropathies

Chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy behaves like a slow GBS — weakness and numbness building over two months or more, often with a stumbling gait and loss of reflexes. It is one of the few genuinely treatable causes of chronic weakness, and it is regularly mistaken for diabetic neuropathy or a spine problem. The diagnosis rests substantially on a carefully performed nerve conduction study.

NervePeripheral neuropathy

Burning, tingling, numbness or pin-prick pain in the feet and hands, often worse at night. Diabetes is the commonest cause, but B12 deficiency, thyroid disease, alcohol, kidney disease, medicines including chemotherapy, hereditary neuropathy (CMT) and vasculitis all produce it. The pattern on examination and nerve conduction tells them apart — and several of these causes are correctable.

NerveSensory ganglionopathy

An uncommon disorder where the sensory nerve cell bodies are attacked, producing clumsy hands, an unsteady gait that worsens in the dark, and numbness that does not follow the usual glove-and-stocking pattern. It is easy to mistake for a balance or spine problem, and it points towards specific autoimmune, drug-related or paraneoplastic causes worth finding.

JunctionMyasthenia gravis

Weakness that comes and goes with use — drooping eyelids and double vision towards evening, a voice that fades during a long conversation, difficulty chewing through a meal, arms that tire while combing hair. It is confirmed with antibody testing and repetitive nerve stimulation, and it is highly treatable. A myasthenic crisis affecting breathing is an emergency, and certain common medicines must be avoided.

CompressionCompressive neuropathy

Carpal tunnel syndrome at the wrist, ulnar nerve compression at the elbow, radial nerve palsy after sleeping on the arm, foot drop from peroneal compression at the knee. Nerve conduction locates the exact site and grades the severity — which decides whether a splint and treatment of the underlying cause will do, or whether surgical release is warranted before permanent damage sets in.

InjuryTraumatic nerve injury

After a fracture, a deep cut, an injection injury or a road accident, nerve conduction and EMG answer the questions surgery depends on: is the nerve cut or only bruised, how much of it is still working, and is it recovering. Timing matters — the study is usually most informative about three weeks after the injury, and repeat studies track whether nerve regrowth is actually happening.

Motor neuronMotor neuron disease

Progressive weakness with muscle wasting, twitching, cramps and — in some — slurred speech or difficulty swallowing, without loss of sensation. Because several treatable conditions imitate it closely, the first duty is to exclude them properly with EMG and the right investigations. Where the diagnosis is confirmed, care focuses on breathing support, nutrition, mobility, communication and symptom relief.

Treatment

What treatment actually looks like

Immunotherapy

IVIg, plasma exchange, steroids and steroid-sparing agents.

Finding the reversible cause

Reversible causes are actively looked for.

Living with the diagnosis

Rehabilitation, surveillance, family screening and honest counselling.
